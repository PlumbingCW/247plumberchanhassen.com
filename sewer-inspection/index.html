What a sewer camera inspection is

.

A sewer camera inspection in Chanhassen is a recorded look inside the pipe that carries waste from your home toward the street. A small camera head on a flexible rod goes in through a cleanout, and a monitor shows the pipe wall in real time. Our 24/7 line takes booking calls any time, though this is a planned diagnostic.

The plumber also carries a locator. The camera head sends a signal that the locator picks up above ground, so the crew can mark the depth and position of anything the camera finds. That marking is what makes a repair plan precise.

What the camera can show

.
  • Roots that have entered through joints.
  • Cracks, breaks and offset or separated joints.
  • A belly or sag where water pools and waste settles.
  • Grease, scale or debris coating the walls.
  • A collapsed section or a bend that has failed.
  • The pipe material, such as cast iron, clay, ABS or PVC, or Orangeburg.

Material matters. Orangeburg, a fiber pipe used in some mid-century housing, softens and deforms over time. Clay joints invite roots, and cast iron rusts from within. Knowing what you have helps you choose between cleaning and repair.

What it cannot show

.

A camera sees a pipe wall, not the soil around it. A leak that does not show water on the lens can be missed. Tiny pipes limit what the head can see, and the area behind the camera is not in view as it moves forward.

A blockage also stops the view. If grease or roots fill the pipe, the plumber has to clear it first with a cable or a jet, then run the camera again. Some inspections become two steps for that reason. Read about clearing on the sewer line cleaning page.

When an inspection pays for itself

.

Some situations call for a look even without symptoms.

Buying a home

A line that looks fine upstairs can be failing underground. A camera view before closing shows what a home inspector cannot see.

Repeat backups

If a cable clearing holds for a few weeks and then fails, the pipe probably has a defect.

Before a remodel

New bathrooms or a basement finish change the loads and the access. Check the line first.

Before anyone digs

A locator and a recording save money by pinpointing the repair so the trench is short.

Not every defect needs work today. A plumber should tell you which findings are urgent, which can wait, and what to watch. If repair is needed, sewer line repair and hydro jetting cover the common paths.

A camera report is easier to act on when you know the yardstick. Minnesota's plumbing code is based on the 2018 Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and inspectors generally care about sound joints, steady slope and pipe that has not collapsed or shifted. Ask the plumber which findings are code concerns and which are plain wear, then check any inspection step with Chanhassen's building inspectors.

Ask for the recording and a written summary

.

A good inspection leaves you with something to keep. Ask for the video file and a short written summary with distances from the cleanout, depth where it was located, and photos of any defects. Without these, you cannot compare quotes.

You can also share the recording with another plumber for a second opinion. That is a fair request, and a reputable shop expects it. Keep the file with your house records for any future sale.

Buying a house: how far an inspection goes

.

A camera view of the sewer line is useful in a purchase, but it is not a substitute for a home inspector. A licensed home inspector looks at the structure, roof, electrical and more. The plumber's camera answers one question: what is the condition of the pipe between the house and the street.

Ask your agent whether the offer can include a sewer inspection contingency. If the camera finds trouble, you can negotiate repairs or a price change. Neighbors in nearby cities such as Eden Prairie face the same question, and the answer is the same: look before you commit.

Local factors that shape the view

.

Most homes here were built after Chanhassen formed as a city in 1967, so many have newer lateral material. Older downtown lots may have older pipe. Your house age and your lot together suggest what the camera might find, so tell the plumber when the home was built.

Chanhassen's city supply goes out hard, about 21 grains per gallon on the city's water page, because the treatment plants stop at iron and manganese. Scale can coat drain pipes, which the camera will show as a rough, crusted wall. That coating is a cue to consider cleaning.

Checking the plumber and the permit

.

A camera visit is plumbing work, so the person running it should be licensed or registered, or employed by a licensed contractor. The state keeps an online license search that takes seconds to use. For any repair that follows, the governing text is Minnesota Rules chapter 4714.

The inspection alone rarely needs a permit. If the footage leads to a dig, call 811 first, and have the city's inspections office weigh in on any permit for the repair itself. Aging pipe may also lead to repiping discussions.
