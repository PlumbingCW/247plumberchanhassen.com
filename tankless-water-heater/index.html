Tankless water heater basics for a Chanhassen home

.

A tankless water heater in Chanhassen heats water as you use it, with no stored tank. Cold water enters, a gas burner or electric element heats it inside a compact heat exchanger, and the heated water leaves. When you close the tap, the unit shuts off. The 24/7 line covers failures, but most of what tankless owners need is planned care.

Because the water never sits in a big tank, the footprint is small and standby losses drop. The trade is that the heat exchanger has thin passages that hard water can clog. That is the main reason this page spends so much time on maintenance.

Flushing and descaling a tankless heater

.

Of all the work done on a tankless unit, a flush matters most. It dissolves and rinses out the mineral scale that builds inside the heat exchanger. Many manufacturers recommend yearly in hard water areas, so check your manual for your model.

Why Chanhassen water makes this a real chore

.

Chanhassen's supply is groundwater, and treatment takes out nearly all the iron and manganese without touching hardness. The city's published figure is around 21 grains per gallon. Heat drives dissolved calcium and magnesium out of the water, and they cling to the hottest surfaces.

Eden Prairie, just across the line, softens at its treatment plant and averages about 5 grains per gallon, which slows scaling for neighbors there. Identical units may end up on quite different flush schedules, depending on which city's water feeds them.

What a flush involves

.

Isolate the unit

Power or gas is turned off and the service valves on the hot and cold sides are closed.

Connect the loop

Hoses link the service ports to a small pump and a bucket of vinegar or an approved descaling solution.

Circulate

The solution runs through the heat exchanger for the time the manual calls for.

Rinse and clean

Fresh water flushes out the solution. The inlet filter screen is removed and cleaned.

Restore and test

Valves are reopened, air is purged, and the unit is fired up and checked.

Units that have never been flushed may be crusted enough that a single pass does not clear it. A plumber can tell whether the unit can be restored or whether the exchanger has been damaged. If the heater is beyond saving, replacement is the next conversation.

Error codes, lukewarm water and the cold sandwich

.

Tankless heaters report their own troubles on a small display. Codes cover ignition problems, flame loss, exhaust trouble, overheating and flow sensor issues. Your manual lists what each code means, and a photo of the screen is a useful thing to have when you call.

  • A code that clears after a reset and does not return may have been a one-time hiccup.
  • A code that returns within days points to scale, a blocked inlet screen, a venting problem or a failing part.
  • Water that swings between hot and cold during a shower can mean the flow is near the low end of what the unit can handle.
  • A short burst of cold water after a pause is the cold-water sandwich, which comes from the pipe and the heat-up time, not from a fault.

A flow that is too low is sometimes the fix itself. Clogged aerators, a dirty inlet screen or partly closed valves choke the water before it reaches the heat exchanger.

Repair, maintain or replace

.

Most tankless problems fall into three buckets. Scale and clogs respond to flushing and screen cleaning. Sensors, igniters and fans are parts that can be swapped. A cracked or heavily scaled heat exchanger is a big repair and, on an older unit, may tip the balance toward a new heater.

If the heater is leaking, switch off the gas or power, close the cold supply and call. A tankless leak at a fitting is sometimes a quick repair, but a leak from inside the cabinet needs a plumber to look. Smell gas at any point, and the building should be emptied before anyone phones the gas utility or 911.

If the failure is sudden and the home is cold, water heater repair covers both tanks and on-demand units in an emergency.

When to call right away about a tankless heater

.
  • Water is dripping or spraying from the cabinet, and you have closed the cold supply valve and switched off the power or gas.
  • Gas odor or scorch marks near the unit: step outside, ring the gas utility or 911, and bring in a plumber only afterward.
  • The display shows a repeating exhaust or flame error, because a blocked vent can let combustion gases back into the room.
  • Hot water has stopped in freezing weather, since pipes near an exterior wall can split before the house warms up again.
  • The unit makes a loud banging or rumbling sound that gets worse each day, which often means heavy scale.

Softening the water ahead of a tankless heater

.

City guidance favors a home softener for most residents. For a tankless unit, a softener or a scale-reducing filter can stretch the time between flushes and protect the exchanger. It does not replace maintenance, but it makes each service easier. Ask the plumber about softening and filtration when you book the install or the first flush.

Tankless installs and repairs sit under chapter 4714 of Minnesota Rules, the state plumbing code adapted from the 2018 UPC. A permit may be needed when a gas line or vent changes, so check with city inspectors first. A plumber's license can be confirmed in a quick search of the state's license lookup.
